About Us

Who We Are

The Space Rangers are a team of engineering and computer science students at Glendale Community College participating in NASA's ASCEND program — Aerospace STEM Challenges to Educate New Discoverers. Through ASCEND, we work together on a hands-on aerospace engineering project where we design, build, test, and prepare a scientific payload for a high-altitude weather balloon flight. Our team works with electronics, programming, data collection, system design, and payload construction while developing real-world engineering and problem-solving skills.

Our Payload

Our Payload

The science, the hardware, and the engineering behind what we're sending to the stratosphere.

Mission

Mission Objective

Design, build, and fly a scientific payload aboard a high-altitude weather balloon to collect atmospheric data at altitudes up to approximately 100,000 feet, and compare our measurements against established atmospheric models.

Scientific Question

"How do temperature, pressure, and humidity change with altitude through the troposphere and into the stratosphere, and how closely do our measurements match the International Standard Atmosphere model?"

Experiment

Our Experiment

Our payload will continuously log temperature, barometric pressure, and relative humidity from ground level through the stratosphere. By comparing our recorded data against the ISA model, we can evaluate sensor accuracy at extreme conditions and identify any atmospheric anomalies during our specific flight.

Hypothesis

We expect temperature to decrease at approximately 6.5°C per 1,000 meters through the troposphere, then stabilize or slightly increase in the lower stratosphere. Pressure will decrease exponentially with altitude. Humidity will drop sharply above the tropopause.

Hardware

Payload Overview

Our payload is a compact, lightweight enclosure housing a sensor suite, microcontroller, and data logger. It is designed to survive temperatures as low as -60°C, fit within the program size and weight limits, and return safely via parachute after balloon burst.

Components

Sensors & Components

Sensors

BME280 Sensor

Measures temperature, barometric pressure, and relative humidity. Accurate to ±1°C and ±1 hPa.

Sensors

GPS Module

Tracks altitude, latitude, longitude, and ground speed throughout the flight.

Microcontroller

Arduino Nano

Main processing unit. Reads all sensors and writes data to the SD card logger at 1-second intervals.

Data Storage

SD Card Logger

Logs all sensor readings with timestamps to a microSD card for post-flight analysis.

Power

3× AA Lithium Batteries

Lithium batteries maintain performance at low temperatures where alkaline batteries fail.

Structure

Foam Enclosure

Lightweight expanded polystyrene enclosure provides thermal insulation and impact protection.

Data

Data We Plan to Collect

Temperature — °C, logged every second from ground to burst altitude
Barometric Pressure — hPa, logged every second
Relative Humidity — %, logged every second
GPS Altitude — meters above sea level, logged every second
GPS Coordinates — Latitude and longitude for flight path mapping
Ground Speed — km/h, for wind drift analysis

Predictions

Expected Results

We expect our data to closely follow the ISA model through the troposphere, with temperature decreasing at the standard lapse rate. We anticipate finding the tropopause — where temperature stops decreasing — at approximately 11–12 km altitude. Humidity should drop to near zero above the tropopause. Any deviations from the model will be the most scientifically interesting part of our analysis.

01Research

Research

Before designing anything, we studied the problem. We reviewed atmospheric science literature, analyzed data from prior ASCEND payloads, and identified the key variables we wanted to measure. Our research established the scientific foundation for every design decision that followed.

02Brainstorming

03Design

Design

Our final payload design uses a BME280 sensor for temperature, pressure, and humidity measurements, a GPS module for altitude tracking, and an Arduino Nano as the central microcontroller. All data is logged to a microSD card at 1-second intervals. The enclosure is fabricated from expanded polystyrene for thermal insulation and impact protection.

05System Design

System Design

The payload system consists of four subsystems: the sensor suite (BME280 + GPS), the processing unit (Arduino Nano), the data storage system (SD card logger), and the power system (3× AA lithium batteries). All subsystems are integrated on a single circuit board inside the foam enclosure.

Add your system block diagram here
06Mechanical Design

Mechanical Design

The payload enclosure is constructed from 1-inch expanded polystyrene foam board, cut and assembled to form a sealed box. The enclosure provides thermal insulation to protect electronics from temperatures as low as -60°C at altitude. A parachute attachment point is integrated into the top of the enclosure.

Add your mechanical drawings or enclosure photos here
07Electrical System

Electrical System

The electrical system centers on the Arduino Nano, which reads the BME280 sensor via I2C and the GPS module via serial communication. Data is written to the SD card logger at 1-second intervals. The system is powered by three AA lithium batteries providing approximately 4.5V, regulated to 3.3V for the sensors.

Add your wiring diagram or circuit board photos here
08Testing

Testing

Our testing plan includes cold chamber testing to simulate high-altitude temperatures, vibration testing to simulate balloon ascent, data logging verification to confirm sensor accuracy, and a drop test to verify parachute deployment. We will document all test results and iterate on any failures before flight.
